1. What this app is for

Swar-Laya Studio is a notebook that plays back what you write. Instead of writing swaras (Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni) on paper and imagining how they sound, you type them into a grid and press ▶ Play — the Studio sings the notes back to you on a chosen instrument, with a tabla or dholak keeping the beat underneath.

It is built around four everyday needs of an Indian-music artist:

ComposeWrite a new bandish, bhajan or film-style tune, swara by swara, and hear it immediately.
Learn / PracticePick a raga and see which notes are its vadi, samvadi and signature phrase (pakkad) — and hear that phrase played.
TranscribeFeed in a recording of a song (yours or someone else's) and get the swara notation back automatically.
Save & shareKeep every composition safely in the cloud, open it on any device, or export it as a file to send to someone.

No musical software experience is assumed anywhere in this guide — every term is explained the first time it's used, and there's a full glossary at the end.

2. Quick start — your first melody in 3 steps

  1. Choose your scale and rhythm. At the top left, pick a Scale (Sa) — this is simply which pitch "Sa" (the home note) will sound at, same idea as picking a key on a harmonium. Then pick a Taal (rhythm cycle) — Keherwa (8 beats) is the simplest and most common for beginners.
  2. Type your first notes. Scroll down to the empty grid under the toolbar. Each beat has a small dropdown — click it and choose a swara (Sa, Re, Ga…). Do this for as many beats as you like.
  3. Press Play. Click ▶ Play. You'll hear your notes played on the harmonium sound, with a tabla keeping Keherwa underneath. Click ■ Stop any time.
That's the whole idea. Everything else in this guide is extra power on top of those three steps — different instruments, rhythms, raga checking, AI help, recording-to-notation, and ways to save your work.

3. The toolbar, explained line by line

The toolbar sits at the top of the screen in three rows. Row 1 sets up the song itself; Row 2 is for playing and composing; Row 3 is for files and the cloud.

Master BPMOverall speed, in beats per minute. Raise it for a faster, livelier feel; lower it for a slow, meditative one.

4. Writing music in the note grid

Each line of your composition is one full cycle of the taal you chose. A line has two rows:

Lyrics row

One small text box per beat — type the syllable of the lyric that falls on that beat, if your song has words. Entirely optional; leave blank for a pure instrumental phrase.

Notes row

One dropdown per beat — choose the swara that sounds on that beat: Sa, re, Re, ga, Ga, Ma, ma, Pa, dha, Dha, ni, Ni (the lowercase/uppercase pairs are the "komal" — flat — and "shuddha" — natural — versions of that note, and tivra Ma is the sharp fourth). Choose - for silence, or ~ to let the previous note continue ringing through this beat instead of re-striking it.

Extra marks on every note

Click the small buttons next to any note to shape exactly how it should sound:

˙˙ / ˙ (above)Taar saptak — sing/play this note one or two octaves higher than the middle register.
• / •• (below)Mandra saptak — one or two octaves lower.
m (meend)Glide smoothly into this note from the previous one, instead of jumping straight to it — the characteristic "slide" of Indian vocal and string music.
+ (add sub-note)Pack more than one note into a single beat — this is how you write a gamak or fast ornamental run (taan), e.g. Ga-Pa-Ga-Dha all inside one beat. Click + again to add more; the × removes one.
Tip: a plain melody is just one note per beat with no marks at all. Octaves, meend and gamak are entirely optional flourishes you add only where the music calls for them — don't feel obliged to use them everywhere.
Want a phrase to lead into, or trail, the first beat (Sam) instead of starting squarely on it? See Entering slightly before or after the Sam in the next section.

5. Per-line tools — giving every line its own character

Every line has its own small settings bar above its grid, so different parts of the same song can sound completely different from one another:

InstrumentWhich sound plays this line's melody: Harmonium, Flute, Sitar, Violin, Piano or Bellpad. Use a different instrument per line to simulate a call-and-response, or an instrumental interlude between vocal lines.
PercussionTabla, Dholak, or Mute (silence) for this line only — mute the percussion for a free, unaccompanied alap-style passage, or switch to Dholak for a folk/Dadra feel.
VolumeThis line's own loudness, relative to the rest — fade a repeated phrase down, or bring a climactic line up.
SectionA free-text label like "Mukhda" (the opening refrain) or "Antara" (the verse that follows it) — purely for your own organisation; it's shown in Bhatkhande View and when browsing saved compositions.
LegatoMakes consecutive notes flow into each other with no gap, instead of each note cutting off sharply — gives a smoother, more "sung" quality.
Adlib/AlapMarks this line as free-tempo: the beat grid becomes flexible (you can add or remove beat columns) and the tabla goes silent — exactly how a singer opens a raga with an unmetered alap before the rhythm comes in.
Line BPMOverrides the Master BPM for just this line — leave blank to follow the master speed, or set a slow number for an Alap line and a faster one for a lively closing line.
▶ LinePlays just this one line on repeat — perfect for rehearsing a tricky phrase in isolation before hearing it in context.
⤴ Insert Before / ×Insert a new blank line just above this one, or delete this line entirely.

Entering slightly before or after the Sam

In Hindustani music a phrase often doesn't begin squarely on Sam — a singer may enter a beat or two before it, letting the melody arrive and resolve exactly on Sam (a common way to open a mukhda), or deliberately hold back and enter just after it. The note grid itself always treats a regular line's very first beat-box as Sam, so there's no single switch for "start 1 beat early" — but both effects are easy to get with tools you already have:

To lead into Sam from just before it

Add a short Adlib/Alap line (see the table above) right before the metered line whose Sam you want to land on. Trim it down to just one or two beats with the − Beat button, set its Line BPM close to the Master BPM so it doesn't feel like a tempo change, and write only your pickup note(s) in it. Because an Adlib line is free-tempo with the tabla silenced, it plays as a brief, unmetered run that flows straight into the next line's Sam — the same shape as a singer's pickup phrase before the beat.

To hold back and enter just after Sam

In the metered line itself, set the Sam beat's note to - (silence) and let the melody's first real note fall on beat 2 or 3 instead. The tabla still marks Sam on time underneath, so the rhythm cycle isn't disturbed — only the melody's entry is delayed, exactly the effect of a singer holding back before coming in.

For a very short flourish right into Sam

Rather than a whole extra line, use the + (add sub-note) button on the last beat before Sam to pack two or three quick notes into that single beat — a gamak run that resolves onto the note landing on Sam. See section 4 for how sub-notes work.

6. The different ways to use this studio

There's no single "correct" way to work. Here are the ways artists actually use the Studio, and when each one fits:

🎼 Mode 1 — Compose from a blank page

Start with nothing, pick a Taal and Scale, and build your bandish or bhajan note-by-note using the grid. Best when you already have a tune in your head and just want to notate and hear it back, refine the rhythm, and try different instruments on different lines before recording for real.

🪔 Mode 2 — Riyaz / raga practice

Turn on Raga Mode, choose a raga, and let the Studio show you which notes belong and play its pakkad (signature phrase) back to you on a chosen instrument. Great for daily practice, teaching a student the shape of a raga, or simply checking "am I allowed to use this note here?" while composing.

🎙 Mode 3 — Transcribe a recording

Already have a melody recorded — your own humming, a student's rendition, an old family recording, or a reference track? Load it into Transcribe and get it turned into editable swara notation automatically, which you can then correct, arrange, and build on. Useful for preserving an oral composition in written form, or for learning a song by seeing its notes rather than only hearing it.

✨ Mode 4 — AI-assisted composition

Short on time, or want a starting idea to react to? Tell AI Compose a raga, a taal, and a mood ("peaceful evening", "devotional morning") and get a full draft composition — with lyrics too, if wanted — that you then edit, re-arrange, and make your own. Works well as a first draft to refine rather than a finished product.

🎚 Mode 5 — Arranging & production sketch

Once the notes exist (written by hand, by AI, or transcribed), use the per-line instrument, percussion, volume and section tools to build a full arrangement: a flute Alap opening, a harmonium Mukhda, a sitar Antara, a tanpura drone underneath it all, with reverb shaping the space. Export the result or keep refining it as a living sketch for a studio recording session.

📖 Mode 6 — Reference & teaching notation

Use Bhatkhande View to display any composition — your own, AI-generated, or transcribed — in the traditional printed layout a student or fellow musician would recognise from a songbook, for teaching or sharing on paper.

7. Raga Mode — composing and checking within a raga

A raga is a set of melodic rules: which notes are allowed, which direction they move in (ascending aroha, descending avaroha), which note is its most important ("vadi") and second-most-important ("samvadi"), and a short characteristic phrase (its "pakkad") that identifies it instantly to a listener.

Turning on the Raga Mode checkbox reveals a raga picker and an information bar showing, for the chosen raga:

Vadi notehighlighted in gold wherever it appears in your notation.
Samvadi notehighlighted in blue.
Out-of-raga notesdimmed, so a note that doesn't belong to the chosen raga stands out visually as worth a second look.
Aroha / Avarohathe raga's official ascending and descending note sequences, shown as reference text.
Pakkadthe signature phrase, shown as a row of note-chips — click ▶ Play next to it (choosing an instrument from the small dropdown first) to hear it performed.
Free vs. Premium: a free account can browse a limited starter set of ragas; signing in with a Premium account unlocks the full library (286 ragas at the time of writing). Raga Mode's highlighting is a guide for the ear and eye, not a hard lock — you can still enter any note you like; it simply tells you when you've stepped outside the raga.

8. Transcribe — turning a recording into notation

Click 🎤 Transcribe to open the panel. The general flow:

  1. Load an audio file (MP3, WAV or OGG) from your computer. A waveform picture of the recording appears.
  2. Mark a section on the waveform — click to set where it starts, then set how many lines (taal cycles) of music that section covers. Give it a label like "Mukhda" if useful.
  3. Click 🔍 Detect Notes. The Studio listens to that section and writes its best guess of the swaras straight into new "draft" lines in your sheet, shown in amber.
  4. Review the draft: play it back, compare it against the original recording with the ▶ Original button, and either ✔ Accept it into your permanent composition, ask for ✨ AI Cleanup to smooth out likely detection mistakes, or ✖ Discard and try again.
  5. Repeat for each section of the recording — Mukhda, Antara, and so on — until the whole song is transcribed.

A few settings worth knowing:

Melody octaveIf every note comes out consistently one octave too high or too low, change this instead of correcting every note by hand.
Energy gateRaise this if tabla or background noise is being mistaken for melody notes; lower it if quiet notes are being missed.
AI backendAn optional, more powerful detection engine that runs on a server instead of in the browser — gives more accurate results on complex recordings, at the cost of needing an internet round-trip. The "Cloud" option works out of the box; "Local" is for a backend running on your own computer.
Ad-lib (sec)If a section opens with a free-tempo alap before the beat properly starts, tell the Studio how many seconds that is — it becomes its own untimed line, with the regular taal lines starting right after it.
Transcription is a very capable first draft, not a perfect transcriber — always listen back and correct a few notes by hand. It saves the vast majority of the manual work, not all of it.

9. AI Compose — let the computer write a tune for you

Click ✨ AI Compose to open the dialog. Choose:

Raga & Taalwhich melodic framework and rhythm cycle to compose in.
Lines to generatehow much music to create — e.g. "4 lines (Sthayi + Antara)" for a short piece with an opening refrain and a verse.
Instrumentwhich sound the generated melody should play on.
Emotion / Subject / Intentiona short free-text description — "morning devotion", "longing for rain", "peaceful evening" — that steers the character of what's generated.
Also generate lyricsoptionally ask for matching lyrics too, in Hindi, Marathi or Sanskrit, fitted to the beat structure.
AI Modelwhich AI service does the writing. Several free options are listed (Google Gemini, Mistral AI, and free community models via OpenRouter) — each needs a free API key from that provider's own website the first time, which the dialog links to directly.

Click ✨ Generate Composition and the result appears as new lines in your sheet — ready to play, edit, re-arrange, or simply use as a seed idea to build something more personal from.

An API key is a free password-like code from the AI provider's own site that lets the Studio talk to it on your behalf — it is never sent anywhere except that provider, and can be remembered on your device so you don't need to paste it in again each time.

10. Saving, loading and sharing your work

There are two independent ways to keep a composition — use either, or both:

📂 File on your own computer (Export / Import Song)

Works without signing in. Export Song downloads the current composition as a small file; Import Song opens one back up. Good for personal backups, or for sending a composition to someone else by email or messaging app — they just need the Studio open to import it too.

☁ Cloud account (Save to Cloud / My Compositions)

Requires signing in once (with Google, or with an email and password — a new account is created automatically the first time). Everything saved this way is tied to your account and reachable from any computer or device, any time, under My Compositions. A free account can keep a small number of compositions; a Premium account has no limit.

Signing in with Google uses your existing Google account — no new password to remember. Signing in with Email creates a brand-new Studio-only account the first time you use a given email and password.

11. Bhatkhande View — traditional printed notation

Click ♪ Bhatkhande View at any time to see the current composition laid out the way it would look printed in an Indian classical-music songbook — one row per taal cycle, with Sam (the first, strongest beat, marked "X") and Khali (the empty beat, marked "0") shown above the notes, exactly as a teacher would write it on a blackboard. This view is read-only — it's for looking and teaching, not editing; close it and go back to the grid to make changes.

12. Tips, shortcuts & troubleshooting

Nothing playsMake sure a Taal has been chosen before adding lines, and that at least one note in the line isn't set to "-" (silence).
Notes sound in the wrong octaveUse the ˙ / • octave buttons on individual notes, or, during transcription, the Melody Octave setting.
"Sign in to use cloud features"Appears when Save to Cloud or My Compositions is used while signed out — sign in once with Google or Email and it won't ask again this session.
Settings reset after closing the browserTaal, BPM, Scale and Master Volume are remembered automatically on the device you used; a fresh browser profile or a different device starts from defaults.
Want more screen spaceUse ⛶ Full Screen to hide the browser's own bars, especially useful on a tablet during a live performance.
Practising one tricky phraseUse the ▶ Line button on just that line instead of playing the whole composition each time.

13. Glossary of terms used in the app

Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni
The seven swaras (notes) of Indian classical music — equivalent in role to Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Ti. Lowercase (re, ga, ma, dha, ni) marks the flat/altered form of that note; Ma uppercase vs lowercase is the one exception, where uppercase "Ma" is natural and lowercase "ma" is the sharp (tivra) form.
Sa / tonic
The "home" note everything else is measured against — set by the Scale (Sa) control.
Taal
A repeating rhythm cycle of a fixed number of beats, e.g. Teentaal has 16.
Theka
The standard pattern of tabla/dholak syllables (bols) that marks out a given Taal's beats.
Sam
The first and strongest beat of a taal cycle, marked "X" on the beat ruler.
Khali
An intentionally "empty" beat within the cycle, marked "0" — traditionally shown with an open hand wave rather than a clap.
Raga
A melodic framework — the set of allowed notes, their typical movement, and a characteristic mood — that a composition is built within.
Thaat
A parent scale that several ragas are grouped under, similar in idea to a Western musical "mode" family.
Vadi / Samvadi
A raga's most important note (vadi) and its second-most-important, often a supporting, note (samvadi).
Aroha / Avaroha
A raga's official ascending and descending note sequence.
Pakkad
A short phrase that instantly identifies a raga to a listener — its melodic "signature".
Jaati
How many of the seven notes a raga uses going up versus coming down (5 = Odav, 6 = Shadav, 7 = Sampurna).
Meend
A smooth glide from one note into the next, rather than a sharp jump — a defining feature of Indian melodic style.
Gamak / Taan
A fast ornamental run of several notes performed within a very short span of time (often a single beat).
Alap
A slow, free-tempo, unaccompanied opening exploration of a raga's notes, before the rhythm begins.
Mukhda / Antara
The opening refrain of a composition (Mukhda) and the verse section that follows and contrasts with it (Antara).
Tanpura
A drone instrument that sustains the Sa (and usually Pa) continuously underneath a performance, giving the singer or player a constant pitch reference.
BPM
Beats Per Minute — how many beats go by in one minute; the standard way to describe musical speed.
